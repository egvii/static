```section
subtitle: Based on use, stage of production, and durability
```

### Based on Use: Final vs. Intermediate Goods

```cards
### Final Goods (teal)
Goods that have crossed the production boundary and are ready for final use – by consumers (consumption) or producers (investment/fixed assets).  
*Example: Bread bought by a household, machinery bought by a factory.*  
✅ Included in National Income.

---
### Intermediate Goods (maroon)
Goods still within the production boundary, to be resold, or used as raw material / input to produce other goods within the same year.  
*Example: Flour bought by a bakery, raw cotton for a textile mill.*  
❌ Not included in National Income (to avoid double counting).
```

### Based on Durability: Consumer Goods vs. Capital Goods

```grid
Consumer Goods – Durable
Used for several years (TV, refrigerator, car).
---
Consumer Goods – Semi‑durable
Used for a year or slightly more (clothes, shoes).
---
Consumer Goods – Non‑durable / Single‑use
Consumed in one use (milk, bread, petrol).
---
Services
Non‑material services (doctor, lawyer, teacher).
---
Capital Goods
Fixed assets of producers used over many years to produce other goods (machinery, tools, equipment).
```

> **Note:** All capital goods are producer goods, but not all producer goods are capital goods (e.g., raw materials are single‑use producer goods, not capital goods).

```section
color: cyan
number: 2
title: Investment
subtitle: Addition to capital stock
```

```callout
type: info
title: Definition
Investment refers to the addition to the capital stock of an economy during a given period – creation of new physical assets (machinery, buildings, raw materials, infrastructure) that help in further production.
```

**Formula:**  
**Total Investment (Gross Domestic Capital Formation) = Fixed Investment + Inventory Investment (Change in Stock)**

### Types of Investment

```grid
Fixed Investment
Addition to fixed assets (machinery, equipment, factory buildings). Increases production capacity.
---
Inventory Investment
Change in stock of finished goods, semi‑finished goods (work‑in‑progress), and raw materials with producers over a year.
```

### Key Macroeconomic Concepts

```note
Gross Investment | Total addition to capital before deducting depreciation.
Net Investment | Gross Investment minus Depreciation (wear and tear of capital).
Formula        | Net Investment = Gross Investment – Depreciation.
```

> **Important:** Simply buying existing financial assets (stocks, bonds) from another person is **not** considered real economic investment – it is merely a transfer of financial claims and does not create new physical capital.

**Other name:** Capital formation.

---

```section
color: purple
number: 3
title: Depreciation
subtitle: Loss of value of fixed capital assets
```

```callout
type: warning
title: Definition
Depreciation is the loss of value of fixed capital assets (machinery, equipment, buildings) over time due to normal wear and tear, accidental damage, and expected (foreseen) obsolescence (technological changes).
```

**Other names:** Consumption of Fixed Capital or Replacement Cost.

```highlight
color: amber
icon: ⚠️
title: Important
Depreciation only accounts for expected / normal obsolescence. Unexpected losses (floods, earthquakes, sudden market crashes) are **capital losses** and are **not** included in depreciation.
```

### Depreciation Reserve Fund

```callout
type: success
title: Depreciation Reserve Fund
A special fund created by a producer or firm to set aside a portion of earnings regularly to replace worn‑out or obsolete fixed assets when they complete their working life.  
It ensures uninterrupted production without sudden external borrowing.
```

**Purpose:** Maintains the existing capital stock and facilitates replacement investment.

### Expected Obsolescence

```note
Definition | Loss of value when an asset becomes outdated due to technological advancements or demand shifts that were reasonably anticipated.  
Role       | Primary component of depreciation.
```

```section
color: indigo
number: 4
title: Stock vs. Flow Concept
```

```grid
Stock Concept
Measured at a particular point in time; no time dimension.  
Examples: Wealth, capital, population, money supply, water in a tank, bank balance.
---
Flow Concept
Measured over a specified period (per hour, per month, per year); has a time dimension.  
Examples: Income, expenditure, investment, savings, depreciation, water flowing from a tap, birth rate.
```

```section
title: Circular Flow of Income
```

### Two‑Sector Economy

```callout
type: info
title: Definition
The circular flow of income in a two‑sector economy represents the continuous flow of production, income generation, and expenditure between **Households** (consumers) and **Firms** (producers).
```

**Assumptions:**  
- Only two sectors: Households and Firms.  
- Closed economy (no foreign trade).  
- No government interference.  
- Households spend entire income on consumption (no savings).

**Three Phases:**

```timeline teal
# Circular Flow Phases
Generation Phase | Firms produce goods and services using factor services from households.
Distribution Phase | Firms distribute income to households as factor payments (rent, wages, interest, profit).
Disposition Phase | Households spend all income to buy goods and services from firms, returning money to firms.
```

### Three‑Sector Economy (with Government)

```highlight
color: purple
icon: 🏛️
title: Adding the Government Sector
Extends the two‑sector model by including government.
```

**Key Flows:**

```grid
Government & Households
Households pay direct taxes (income tax) to government.  
Government gives transfer payments (pensions, scholarships, subsidies) and public services.
---
Government & Firms
Firms pay corporate and indirect taxes.  
Government buys goods and services (defence, infrastructure) and gives production subsidies.
```

**Leakages & Injections:**  
- **Leakages:** Taxes and saving.  
- **Injections:** Government spending and transfer payments.

---

### Real Flow vs. Money Flow

```grid
Real Flow (Physical Flow)
Flow of factor services (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) from households to firms, and the corresponding flow of final goods and services from firms to households – without involvement of money.  
Determines real output and production capacity.
---
Money Flow (Nominal Flow)
Flow of money (factor payments – rent, wages, interest, profit – and consumption expenditure) between households and firms.  
Money acts as a medium of exchange; facilitates transactions.
```

```section
number: 6
title: Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply
```

### Aggregate Demand (AD)

```callout
type: info
title: Definition
AD is the total demand for final goods and services in an economy at a given price level during a specific period. It represents total spending in the economy.
```

**Components (four‑sector economy):**  
**AD = C + I + G + (X – M)**  
(Consumption + Investment + Government Spending + Net Exports)

**AD Curve:** Downward‑sloping from left to right – inverse relationship between price level and quantity of real GDP demanded.

### Aggregate Supply (AS)

```callout
type: info
title: Definition
AS is the total supply of final goods and services that producers are willing and able to produce and sell at a given price level during a specific period. It represents the productive capacity of the economy.
```

**Shape depends on time horizon:**

```grid
Short‑Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)
Upward sloping. Some prices (especially wages) are sticky. Higher price level leads to higher profits and more output.
---
Long‑Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)
Vertical straight line at potential GDP (full employment). Unaffected by price level; determined by resources (labour, capital) and technology.
```

### Short‑Run & Long‑Run Equilibrium

```note
Short‑Run Equilibrium | AD = SRAS. Can occur at, above, or below full employment (inflationary or recessionary gap).
Long‑Run Equilibrium  | AD = SRAS = LRAS. Occurs at full employment output (potential GDP); cyclical unemployment is zero.
```

```section
color: teal
number: 7
title: Consumption Function
```

```callout
type: info
title: Definition
Shows the functional relationship between total consumption expenditure (C) and total disposable income (Y). It explains planned consumption at various income levels.
```

**Algebraic Equation (Linear Consumption Function):**  
**C = c̄ + bY**

- **c̄** = Autonomous Consumption – minimum consumption even when income is zero (using past savings or borrowing).  
- **b** = Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC), where 0 < b < 1 – fraction of additional income spent on consumption.  
- **Y** = Total Income.

**Keynes’s Psychological Law of Consumption:**  
As income increases, consumption increases, but **not by as much** as the increase in income (a portion is saved).

---

### MPC and MPS

```note
MPC (Marginal Propensity to Consume) | ΔC / ΔY ; 0 < MPC < 1 ; fraction of extra income spent.
MPS (Marginal Propensity to Save)   | ΔS / ΔY ; 0 < MPS < 1 ; fraction of extra income saved.
Relationship | MPC + MPS = 1.
```

> **Note:** Inverse relation between expenditure and saving.

### Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Sectors

```cards
### Primary Sector (red)
Agriculture and allied activities – extraction/harvesting of natural resources.  
Examples: crop cultivation, forestry, fishing, animal husbandry, mining.  
<span style="color:red;">Red collar jobs.</span>
Growth: 3.1% (boosted by favourable monsoon). Allied activities (livestock 7.1%, fisheries 8.8%) outpaced traditional crops.
---
### Secondary Sector (blue)
Manufacturing and industry – transforming raw materials into finished/semi‑finished goods.  
Includes manufacturing, construction, electricity, gas, water supply.  
<span style="color:blue;">Blue collar jobs.</span>
Industrial GVA grew 6.2%, manufacturing 7.0%. PLI schemes attracted ₹2 lakh crore investments, generated ₹18.7 lakh crore production, created 12.6 lakh jobs.
---
### Tertiary Sector
Services – intangible services rather than physical goods.  
Includes trade, transport, financial services, real estate, IT, consultancy.  
<span style="color:green;">White collar jobs.</span>
Expanded 9.1% – led by financial services, real estate, professional services. India is world’s 7th‑largest services exporter (4.3% global share) and largest recipient of FDI inflows.
```
